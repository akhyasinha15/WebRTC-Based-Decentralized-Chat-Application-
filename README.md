The contemporary digital landscape is characterized by an unprecedented level of 
interconnectedness, facilitated largely by real-time communication (RTC) platforms. These 
systems, ranging from instant messaging applications to large-scale video conferencing services, 
have become indispensable tools for personal, professional, and societal interaction. However, 
the architectural foundation of the vast majority of these platforms is centralized. This model, 
wherein all communication, metadata, and user data are routed through and stored on servers 
controlled by a single corporate entity, presents a series of fundamental and increasingly 
untenable weaknesses. 
Centralized architectures are inherently fragile, creating single points of failure that can lead to 
widespread service outages if the central server infrastructure fails.1 More critically, this 
concentration of data and control creates significant privacy and security vulnerabilities. Users 
are compelled to place their trust in service providers to safeguard their personal information, a 
trust that has been repeatedly undermined by data breaches, unauthorized data monetization, and 
exploitation of user data for commercial purposes.2 Furthermore, the centralized control model is 
susceptible to censorship and surveillance, as governments can compel service providers to block 
content or provide access to user communications, a particularly acute threat in environments 
with restrictive political regimes.3 This paradigm effectively forces users to relinquish 
sovereignty over their own data and conversations, creating a power imbalance that is 
fundamentally at odds with the principles of a free and open internet. The lack of trust in these 
centralized monopolies makes their platforms unsuitable for mission-critical and highly sensitive 
use cases, such as military or governmental operations, where data integrity and confidentiality 
are paramount
